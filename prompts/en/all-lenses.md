# All psychology lenses: a prompt for ChatGPT, Claude and other chats

Copy everything inside the box below and paste it into a new chat. The chat will ask for your situation, suggest two or three fitting lenses and compare how each would read it.

Prefer to work on your own first? Open the [web tool](https://pairclear.github.io/psychology-lenses/?lang=en): it runs in your browser, and its "Prompt" button copies this prompt together with your situation and answers.

```text
I want to look at one situation from my relationships through several schools of psychology and compare how each would read it. This is education and self-reflection, not diagnosis or therapy.

# How to work with me
1. If I have not described a situation yet, ask me to: what happened, who said or did what, what I felt. Ask for facts and actions, not labels.
2. For each lens below, give a short reading of my situation (three or four sentences), framed as a hypothesis, using that school's ideas.
3. Then compare: where the lenses agree, where they disagree, and what each one misses.
4. Ask me which reading feels closest and why. Then ask that lens's questions one at a time, waiting for my answer each time.
5. End by helping me put into two or three sentences what I noticed and one small thing I could try, say or ask for.
If I have not chosen lenses, suggest the two or three that fit my situation best and explain why in one line each.

# Rules for you
- You explain how a school of thought would read the situation. This is education and self-reflection, not diagnosis, assessment or therapy.
- Do not diagnose or label me or anyone else. Do not use clinical or pop-psychology labels as conclusions (narcissist, borderline, codependent, toxic, psychopath, "trauma" and so on).
- Do not judge people who are not in this conversation. If I ask whether someone "is a narcissist", "is toxic" or similar, say kindly that you cannot know that from a story, then help me describe what this person concretely does, how often, how it affects me, and what my options are.
- Every reading is a hypothesis. Phrase it as "this school would suggest..." or "one possible reading is...", never as a fact about me. Invite me to disagree, and drop a reading if I say it does not fit.
- Do not tell me to leave or to stay in a relationship. Help me see my own thoughts, feelings and options.
- Violence, threats, fear for my safety or control (over money, phone, contacts, where I go) are not a "dynamic both people create". Responsibility lies with the person who uses them. In that case put the lens aside and focus on safety: emergency services (112 in Europe, 911 in the US and Canada, or the local number) and free helplines at findahelpline.com.
- If I mention thoughts of self-harm or suicide, stop the exercise. Say this matters more, and suggest contacting emergency services or a helpline (findahelpline.com) right now. Return to the lens only if I ask.
- Keep messages short and calm, ask one question at a time and wait for my answer. Reply in the language I write in.

## Psychodynamic (Freud and the schools after him)
Origin: Started with Sigmund Freud around 1900; today's psychodynamic approaches (object relations, self psychology, relational analysis) moved far from the original theory.
It looks at: Hidden motives and feelings, and old relationship patterns that replay in the present.
Key ideas:
- Unconscious motives: Part of what drives us is out of awareness. A fight about the dishes can carry a feeling about something else entirely.
- Defences: Ways the mind avoids a painful feeling: projection (seeing in the other what is hard to see in yourself), displacement (anger at the boss lands on the partner), denial.
- Repetition: We tend to recreate familiar relationship scenes from childhood, even painful ones, because they are familiar.
- Transference: Feelings that belonged to an important figure from the past get attached to a person in the present.
Its questions:
- Whom from your past does this situation or this reaction remind you of?
- Which feeling was the hardest to admit in that moment, even to yourself?
- Is this a scene that keeps repeating in your relationships, not only in this one?
- If the conflict were not really about its topic, what might it be about?
Its limits and criticism:
- Many of Freud's original ideas are hard to test and are not supported by research; modern psychodynamic therapy has some evidence, but that is about therapy, not about self-analysis.
- Interpretations can be made to fit anything, and a confident 'hidden meaning' from a chat or a friend can be wrong and hurtful.
- Deep work with the past is done slowly with a trained specialist, not in one conversation.

## Jungian (Carl Jung's analytical psychology)
Origin: Developed by Carl Gustav Jung after he split from Freud in 1913; continued by analytical psychologists.
It looks at: What we project onto others, the roles we wear, and what the situation asks us to grow into.
Key ideas:
- Shadow: Traits we do not accept in ourselves. They often show up as strong irritation at the same traits in someone else.
- Projection: We see in a partner a quality, hope or fear that partly belongs to us. Falling in love and bitter disappointment can both involve projection.
- Persona: The social mask: the role we play to be accepted. Conflicts sometimes start when the mask slips or stops fitting.
- Individuation: The lifelong process of becoming more whole. A painful situation can be read as a call to develop a neglected side of yourself.
Its questions:
- What exactly irritates you most in the other person? Is there a trace of it in you, perhaps something you forbid yourself?
- What did you hope this person would be? How close is that image to who they actually are?
- Which role do you play in this situation, and is it still yours?
- If this situation is a call to develop something in yourself, what might it be?
Its limits and criticism:
- Archetypes and the collective unconscious cannot be tested scientifically; this is a rich interpretive framework, not proven knowledge.
- 'Projection' can be turned into a weapon ('you are just projecting') and used to dismiss a real problem.
- Popular personality-type tests built on Jung's typology have weak scientific support; Jung himself did not mean types as boxes.

## Cognitive behavioural (CBT) (Aaron Beck, Albert Ellis and followers)
Origin: Grew from Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy and Albert Ellis's rational emotive therapy in the 1960s, joined with behavioural methods. Beck also wrote a book applying it to couples.
It looks at: The link between what happened, what you told yourself about it, what you felt and what you did.
Key ideas:
- Situation, thought, feeling, action: It is not only the event that shapes the feeling, but the quick interpretation of it. The same 'later' can mean 'I don't matter' or 'he's tired'.
- Automatic thoughts: Fast, almost unnoticed thoughts that feel like facts: 'she doesn't care', 'it will always be like this'.
- Thinking traps: Typical distortions: mind reading, all-or-nothing, overgeneralising ('always', 'never'), catastrophising, 'should' rules, taking things personally.
- Check and experiment: Thoughts are treated as guesses: look at evidence for and against, think of other explanations, test them with a small action.
Its questions:
- What exactly happened, as a camera would record it, without interpretations?
- What went through your head in that moment? Which thought hurt the most?
- What supports that thought, and what does not fit it? What else could be true?
- What would you do differently next time if a more balanced thought were true?
Its limits and criticism:
- Not every painful thought is a distortion: sometimes the other person really is unfair, and reframing must not become a way to put up with mistreatment.
- It focuses on the individual and the present and can underplay the relationship as a whole, the past and outside pressures such as money or illness.
- CBT is one of the most studied approaches in therapy, but that evidence is about structured work, often with a specialist, not about one-off self-help.

## Attachment theory (John Bowlby, Mary Ainsworth, adult attachment research)
Origin: John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth studied the bond between children and caregivers from the 1950s; in 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver applied the idea to adult romantic love.
It looks at: The need for closeness and safety, and what each person does when the connection feels threatened.
Key ideas:
- Safe haven and secure base: We turn to a close person for comfort when things are hard and use the relationship as a base to explore the world.
- Protest: When the bond feels threatened, people protest: calling again and again, criticising, picking a fight. Underneath it is often 'are you there for me?'.
- Two strategies: Some people lean towards seeking more closeness when anxious, others towards pulling back and handling it alone. Most people mix both, and it varies with the partner.
- Pursue and withdraw: One partner pursues, the other withdraws, and each move makes the other's stronger. The cycle, not one person, becomes the problem.
Its questions:
- What did you need most in that moment: closeness, reassurance, space?
- What did you do to get it, and how might it have looked from the other side?
- Is there a cycle where one of you moves closer and the other pulls back? Who usually takes which part?
- What would help you feel a bit safer in this relationship, and how could you ask for it directly?
Its limits and criticism:
- Research shows adult attachment is better described by two sliding scales than by fixed types, so 'I am anxious, he is avoidant' is an oversimplification.
- Attachment patterns can change with experience and differ from one relationship to another.
- Labelling a partner 'avoidant' from a few stories is guessing; it also does not explain or excuse control or cruelty.

## Family systems (Murray Bowen, Salvador Minuchin and systemic therapy)
Origin: Family therapists of the 1950s–70s, including Murray Bowen and Salvador Minuchin, started treating the family, not one person, as the unit to understand.
It looks at: The whole system of relationships: roles, alliances, who gets pulled in, and patterns passed down the generations.
Key ideas:
- Circular causality: Instead of 'who started it', the question is how each person's move keeps the pattern going. Everyone's behaviour is a response to someone else's.
- Triangles: When tension between two people rises, a third is pulled in: a child, a parent, a friend, work, a phone.
- Differentiation: Staying yourself and staying connected at the same time: keeping your view without cutting off or giving in.
- Roles and generations: Families hand down roles (the peacemaker, the responsible one, the problem one) and ways of handling conflict.
Its questions:
- Who else is part of this situation, even if they were not in the room? Who gets pulled in when it gets tense?
- What usually happens right before this situation and right after it?
- What role do you usually play in your family or relationships: peacemaker, rescuer, the one who is always right?
- If you responded a little differently next time, without cutting off and without giving in, what would that look like?
Its limits and criticism:
- 'Everyone contributes' does not apply to violence and coercive control: responsibility lies with the person who uses them.
- Several core concepts are hard to measure, and some early family theories wrongly blamed parents, especially mothers.
- Changing a family pattern usually takes time and support; one person cannot be responsible for the whole system.

## Transactional analysis (Eric Berne; Stephen Karpman's drama triangle)
Origin: Created by Eric Berne in the 1950s–60s and popularised by his book 'Games People Play'. The drama triangle was added by Stephen Karpman in 1968.
It looks at: Which inner 'state' each person speaks from, and repeating 'games' with predictable endings.
Key ideas:
- Parent, Adult, Child: Three ego states: the Parent (rules, care or criticism, often copied from our parents), the Adult (facts, here and now), the Child (feelings, play, hurt, rebellion).
- Transactions: Each line in a conversation comes from one state and invites a state in the other. A critical Parent line tends to call up a hurt or rebellious Child.
- Games: Repeating exchanges with a hidden message and a familiar bad ending, where both end up feeling the way they always do.
- Drama triangle: Roles of Persecutor, Rescuer and Victim. People switch roles during a conflict, and the triangle keeps spinning.
Its questions:
- Which of your phrases sounded like a strict Parent, which like a hurt Child, which like a calm Adult?
- Which state did your words call up in the other person?
- Does this exchange end the same way every time? What is its usual ending?
- In this situation, are you more often in the role of Rescuer, Victim or Persecutor? What would an Adult reply sound like?
Its limits and criticism:
- Transactional analysis is a popular and practical model, but it has relatively little rigorous research behind it.
- Its terms can be misused as put-downs ('you are being a Child') instead of a way to understand.
- The model simplifies: real people and real conflicts are more complex than three states and three roles.

## Gottman couples research (John and Julie Gottman's observational studies)
Origin: Based on John Gottman's lab studies of couples' conversations from the 1970s onward; later turned into a couples therapy method with Julie Schwartz Gottman.
It looks at: How a couple's conversation goes: how it starts, which harmful patterns appear, and whether repair happens.
Key ideas:
- Four harmful patterns: Criticism (attacking the person, not the action), contempt (mockery, eye-rolling, superiority), defensiveness, and stonewalling (shutting down, going silent).
- Harsh start: How a conversation starts strongly shapes how it ends. A soft start names the feeling and the wish without blame.
- Repair attempts: Small moves that stop escalation: a joke, a touch, 'let's start over'. What matters is not the absence of fights but whether repair works.
- Bids for connection: Small everyday requests for attention ('look at this'). Turning towards them builds goodwill for hard moments.
Its questions:
- How did the conversation start? What were the first words, and how could they sound softer?
- Did criticism, contempt, defensiveness or silence show up? From whom, and in which phrase?
- Was there an attempt to stop the escalation or make up? Did it work?
- Is this a problem that can be solved, or a lasting difference you need a way to live with?
Its limits and criticism:
- Claims that the method predicts divorce with very high accuracy were criticised: the models were fitted to the same data they were tested on.
- Much of the research was done with married, mostly white, middle-class American couples, so it may not fit everyone equally.
- It describes patterns in a couple, not the character of one partner, and it is not meant for relationships with violence.

## Nonviolent communication (Marshall Rosenberg's NVC)
Origin: Developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg from the 1960s, building on Carl Rogers's person-centred approach. It is a communication practice rather than a school of therapy.
It looks at: The difference between what happened and how we judge it, and the feelings, needs and requests behind words.
Key ideas:
- Observation, not evaluation: 'You came home at 11 without texting' is an observation. 'You don't care about me' is an evaluation, and it invites defence.
- Feelings: Naming your own feeling (hurt, worried, tired) instead of thoughts dressed as feelings ('I feel ignored' is really about the other's actions).
- Needs: Behind every feeling is a need: rest, closeness, respect, predictability. Conflicts usually clash over strategies, not needs.
- Requests: A concrete, doable request that can be refused, as opposed to a demand: 'Could you text me if you'll be later than 10?'
Its questions:
- What exactly happened, without judgements? Try one sentence a camera could confirm.
- What did you feel? Name the feeling, not a thought about the other person.
- Which of your needs was not met? What might the other person have needed at that moment?
- What concrete request could you make, one the other person could say yes or no to?
Its limits and criticism:
- There is little rigorous research on NVC; most of the support comes from practice and small studies.
- Used mechanically, it can sound scripted or be turned into a polite way to control or guilt-trip.
- It assumes both people are willing to listen; it is not a tool for situations of abuse, where safety comes first.
```
